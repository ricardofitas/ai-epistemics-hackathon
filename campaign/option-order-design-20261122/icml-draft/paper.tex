\begin{abstract}
How should two models share a limited sample of answer orders when
estimating their mean accuracy gap? With $n$ options and $n$ shared
orders, exact position balance incurs worst-case variance of at least
$(n-2)/(n^2-3n+1)$ times the full-order variance for every $n\ge4$.
Explicit samplers attain the bound for $6\le n\le10$. Covering every
directed adjacency retains single-order variance at four and six
options. At four options we obtain the exact minimax risks for
every position-balanced budget from four through 24 calls. At eight
calls, adding adjacency to precedence balance raises the optimum from
$2/11$ to $2/5$. The two optimal designs admit an exact comparison
on any fixed panel through the fraction of residual variance that
survives adjacency balance. Replaying all 24 orders for the same 3B
model pair on HellaSwag, MMLU and LogiQA, we find higher eight-call
point error under adjacency on all three. On LogiQA, adjacency gives
$2.32\%$ lower mean squared error at four calls and $1.69\%$ higher
at eight, with the eight-call interval spanning a tie.
\end{abstract}

\section{Paired comparisons under an order budget}
An evaluator can spend six calls on one question so that every option
appears in every position and every ordered pair of options appears
consecutively. Those six orders can still agree perfectly on whether
two particular answers sit at opposite ends or in other mirrored slots.
If the correctness difference depends only on that relation, its
six-call mean has the same variance as a single uniform draw. We ask which
evaluation designs admit this failure and what precision can be
guaranteed with the same budget.

For each of $N$ questions, hold the wording, semantic options and
answer key fixed. Let $d_i(\pi)$ be model A's correctness minus model
B's correctness at display order $\pi$. The answer label moves with
the correct option. The target is the all-order accuracy gap
\[
 \Delta=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\mu_i,\qquad
 \mu_i=(n!)^{-1}\sum_{\pi\in S_n}d_i(\pi).
\]
On each question the models share an independently drawn block of $k$
distinct, equally weighted orders. Each order has inclusion probability
$k/(n!)$, so the estimate is unbiased. We study the budget $k=n$ across
option counts and every budget admitting exact position balance at $n=4$.
The evaluations use all orders as the finite-panel reference.

Exact balance integrates specified features without sampling error.
It also restricts which orders can be observed together. We give a
general lower bound with explicit optimal samplers and a connectivity
criterion for the largest possible loss from these restrictions.
At five options we bound the cost of maximum feasible adjacency.
At four, doubling the budget changes both the optimal samplers and
the observed ordering of their risks. The fixed-budget statements concern arbitrary order
profiles. The experiments measure where actual profiles place their
variance and explain the sign changes between benchmarks.

\section{Four options across order budgets}
At four calls, encode an order by the map from original option to display position.
Compose a uniform anchor with each map in the subgroup
\(\{0123,1302,3210,2031\}\). Relabeling the options by the anchor yields six
equiprobable, disjoint blocks. Every block places each option once in each
position, has two display reversal pairs, and contains each directed
adjacency once. The corresponding design integrates the span of additive
position, pair precedence and directed-adjacency features. Its mean is
unbiased for any correctness contrast because each order is included
with probability \(1/6\). Shared evaluation of the two models yields a
single contrast for each displayed order.

For an independent block \(B_i\) per item, the exact conditional risk is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat\Delta)=
N^{-2}\sum_i \mathbb E_{B_i}(\bar d_{i,B_i}-\mu_i)^2.
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
We evaluate it by enumerating the block support. Ordinary cyclic
blocks balance each position. Uniform balanced blocks sample from all 24
position-balanced quadruples. We also compare four unrestricted distinct
orders, two distinct reversal pairs, and the double-transposition design.
All schedules share the four-call budget and the two models use the same
orders. Exact risk uses all 24 observed responses to evaluate the
proposed four-call deployment.

A model choice based on the sign of the estimated gap has a separate
error criterion. For a fixed panel with $\Delta\ne 0$, we report
$\Pr(\widehat\Delta\Delta<0)$, with exact ties reported separately.
For independent item blocks, the distribution of the sum is obtained
by convolving the discrete block scores for each item. Thus changes in
MSE can matter even on panels where no sampled design can reverse the
ranking. We treat practical gap thresholds as distinct decisions.

\subsection{The minimax cost of balance}
For one item write \(v=\sum_\pi(d(\pi)-\mu)^2/24\). For a uniform
design on \(m\) blocks with block-incidence matrix \(H\), the normalized
worst-case risk on real centered contrasts is the largest centered
eigenvalue of \(24H^\top H/(k^2m)\).
Table~\ref{tab:frontier} gives the optimum over designs satisfying each
constraint on every realized block. The unrestricted column follows from
simple random sampling. At four calls, averaging a position-balanced design
over relabelings can only reduce its worst-case risk and leaves two
block orbits of sizes 18 (cyclic type) and six (double-transposition type).
A centered ternary witness has respective risks \(1/2\) and \(1\),
giving a lower bound for all mixtures. Uniform sampling from the 18
cyclic-type blocks attains \(1/2\).
Precedence balance leaves two cyclic families and the double-transposition family. The
uniform union of the two cyclic families attains \(3/4\), and a ternary witness gives the same
lower bound for the cyclic families and a larger bound for double transpositions. Directed adjacency leaves only the six
Williams blocks. A contrast constant on each block
gives the sharp risk of one. Appendix~\ref{app:certificates} gives the
matrix and certificate construction.

Sharp worst-case risks allow a low-variance explanation for balanced
sampling in practice. If the features balanced by a design explain
a fraction \(\eta\) of centered variance, only its orthogonal residual
contributes to risk and the normalized risk is at most
\(\lambda(1-\eta)\), where \(\lambda\) is the corresponding ratio in
Table~\ref{tab:frontier}. At four calls this gives sufficient shares of \(13/23\),
\(49/69\) and \(18/23\) for the three balanced policies to beat
unrestricted four. It does not order their residual risks.

\subsection{Eight calls and the full budget curve}
\label{sec:budget}
\paragraph{Theorem 3 (four options, variable budget).}
For every positive multiple of four up to 24, the minimax risks for
uniform-marginal sampling without replacement are those of
Table~\ref{tab:frontier}. At eight and twelve calls each balanced
optimum is attained by a sampler with equal variance in every
direction of the span of centered feasible block indicators.
Appendix~\ref{app:budgets} gives a certificate applicable to any finite
population and the explicit four-option samplers.

At eight calls every option occupies each slot twice. Adding
precedence requires four occurrences of each orientation. Adding
adjacency requires two of every directed neighbor pair. Only 15
blocks meet all three requirements. They are exactly the unions of
two different Williams blocks from the six that partition the 24
orders. Drawing two uniformly without replacement multiplies the
four-call Williams MSE by $2/5$ on \emph{every} fixed panel.
For precedence, pairing two disjoint four-call minimax blocks uniformly
has worst-case ratio $3/14$. Optimizing the eight-call design gives
$2/11$. The difference can therefore matter at equal inference cost.

There is also an exact test of which eight-call design wins on a fixed
panel. Let $E_P$ be the summed squared length of item profiles after
orthogonal projection away from the constant, position and precedence
features. Let $E_W$ be the summed squared length retained by replacing
each centered item profile with its within-Williams-block means.
These subspaces have dimensions 11 and five, respectively, with the
second contained in the first. The eight-call MSEs are
$(2/11)E_P/(24N^2)$ and $(2/5)E_W/(24N^2)$. Thus adjacency improves
on precedence exactly when
\begin{equation}
 E_W/E_P < 5/11 \quad (45.45\%), \qquad E_P>0.
 \label{eq:residual-threshold}
\end{equation}
If $E_P=0$ both risks vanish. The threshold uses full response
profiles, making it a retrospective explanation of the ranking.

\subsection{Decomposing the risk difference}
For an item, let $K_D$ denote the average outer product of the
centered block weights $\mathbf{1}_{B}/4-\mathbf{1}/24$ under design
$D$. Its item risk is $d^\top K_D d$. Both designs balance position
and precedence, so these components and the constant are annihilated.
Project the remaining contrast onto the incremental directed-adjacency
span, giving $a$, and its orthogonal complement, giving $r$. Williams
also balances adjacency within each block, so $K_Wa=0$. With $P$ for
precedence minimax and $W$ for Williams, the item risk difference is
\begin{align}
d^\top(K_P-K_W)d
  &= a^\top K_Pa + r^\top(K_P-K_W)r \notag\\
  &\quad + 2a^\top K_Pr .
\label{eq:risk-components}
\end{align}
The adjacency term is nonnegative. The other terms can have either
sign. Summing over items and dividing by $N^2$ gives the gap MSE difference.

\subsection{What changes between the observed panels}
Figure~\ref{fig:matched-components} isolates the change of benchmark.
On MMLU the reduction in adjacency variance is smaller than the
additional residual variance, even after including their interaction.
On LogiQA the adjacency term alone offsets the residual cost. On
HellaSwag the interaction nearly cancels the difference. The relative
MSE changes are $3.41\%$ higher, $2.32\%$ lower, and $0.25\%$ higher
with Williams, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:3b-confirmation}).
The earlier HellaSwag study with smaller checkpoints gave a $1.17\%$
gain. There the positive interaction was large enough to change the
sign (Figure~\ref{fig:risk-components} in the appendix).

We applied the same decomposition to 17 previously scored panels.
The adjacency contribution favored Williams and the residual
contribution favored precedence minimax on all 17. The interaction
changed sign. These are retrospective diagnostics on the full 24-order
profiles, with repeated prompts and reused cohorts in some panels.
No rule using prompt type or model scale passed a development screen
that excluded the held benchmark and model pair from training.
Appendix~\ref{app:risk-projections} gives projection and replay details.

\section{Frozen full-order evaluations}
\subsection{Preregistered HellaSwag confirmation}
We froze a 5\% reduction in paired MSE against each of four
equal-call comparators as the practical claim. We enrolled 6,144
source-qualified HellaSwag items \citep{zellers2019hellaswag},
3,072 each from the in-domain and zero-shot strata. The roster,
prompt, both model checkpoints, exact reference and analysis
were committed before inference. The run used Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct
and Granite-3.3-2B-Instruct in Q4\_K\_M and selected a single
numeric option token. Both models saw all 24 orders, and one
RTX 4060 served all 16 shards. After independent release of the
complete digit export, the analysis joined the publisher answers
by two source keys. A second implementation independently
reproduced the aggregate results. Appendix~\ref{app:hellaswag}
records the protocol and evidence.

For comparator \(C\), we use the practical margin
\(0.95\operatorname{MSE}_{C}-\operatorname{MSE}_{W}\).
The frozen primary analysis uses two-sided 95\% simultaneous
normal intervals across all four margins, with variance estimated
within each stratum. A separate bounded sensitivity uses the
prespecified score range. Full-order accuracies were 44.15\% for
Qwen and 37.90\% for Granite, a 6.25 percentage-point gap.

\subsection{Frozen 3B confirmation and matched HellaSwag extension}
We compared Qwen2.5 3B and SmolLM3 3B in Q4\_K\_M on 992 questions
each from MMLU validation \citep{hendrycks2021} and English LogiQA
2.0 test \citep{liu2023logiqa}. We fixed the rosters and analysis
before inference. Selection used hashes, deduplication, four valid
choices and a context check across both models and all orders.
The panels were fresh to this experiment. Overlap with model
training corpora is unknown. Both models scored all 24 orders in
the standard numeric prompt on one RTX 4060 Ti. The 95,232
model--item--order calls establish the reference. Each candidate
design uses four shared orders per model and item.

The primary comparison was Williams against precedence minimax at
a 5\% relative MSE reduction. We used 50,000 paired item bootstrap
draws per panel, resampling within subject on MMLU at fixed
observed weights. Bonferroni bounds control the two panels
separately for each one-sided direction. We also computed normal
and bounded sensitivities. The exact finite-panel MSE was checked
through an independent rational calculation of each block's loss.

The HellaSwag extension used the same Q4\_K\_M weights,
numeric prompt template, 512-token admission rule and RTX 4060 Ti.
Its validation panel contains 765 previously unused questions and
227 from the earlier 1.5B/2B HellaSwag run, selected by hash and length
before inference. The template uses the HellaSwag context as the
question and the four endings as options. We scored all 24 orders
and reused the paired bootstrap procedure with fixed in-domain and
zero-shot stratum weights. The new interval is panel-specific.

\subsection{Frozen decision experiment}
On 288 ARC-Challenge and 288 OpenBookQA items, we compared Qwen2.5
1.5B Instruct with Granite 3.3 2B Instruct (Q4\_K\_M) under a
standard numeric-choice prompt. The frozen decision was whether
Qwen led by at least five percentage points. The reference used all
24 orders per model and question. We convolved the exact distributions
of the independently sampled four-order blocks and used 4,000 paired
item bootstrap draws for simultaneous directional bounds across panels.

On ARC-Challenge, the full-order lead was 2.894 points and Williams
reduced decision error from 1.197\% to 1.172\% versus precedence
minimax. Ordinary cyclic yielded 1.471\%. The Williams reduction was
0.025 points, with directional bounds $[-0.255, 0.417]$ points.
OpenBookQA's lead was $-7.002$ points, 12.002 points from the
threshold, and all three decision errors were effectively zero.
The frozen comparison excluded a reduction above one point on both
panels. Only ARC-Challenge was relatively close to the threshold.

\section{Five-option evaluation on CommonsenseQA}
We froze 192 questions from CommonsenseQA validation
\citep{talmor2019commonsenseqa}, using a fixed hash order,
one item per normalized concept and a label-free context check.
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and SmolLM3-3B used the same Q4\_K\_M
checkpoints and RTX 4060 Ti as the four-option 3B panels.
The numeric template used five choices and single-token argmax.
We ran both models on all 120 orders per item, for 46,080
reference calls. Each estimator draws five shared orders per
question, for 1,920 deployment calls across both models.

The primary comparison uses the position-minimax sampler and the
maximum-adjacency sampler certified in Appendix~\ref{app:five-certificates}.
The first design covers 12.7 directed adjacencies on average.
The second covers 15 in every draw. We enumerated each design's
weighted block risk and used 50,000 paired item bootstrap draws.

\section{Relation to earlier work and scope}
Williams designs balance first-order carryover in experiments
\citep{williams1949}. The catalogues used here classify row-complete Latin
squares \citep{wanlesscatalogue}. Balanced survey sampling integrates
auxiliary totals \citep{deville2004}. Orthogonal-array sampling
stratifies margins in product spaces \citep{owen1992}, and
order-of-addition designs optimize information for models of component
precedence \citep{peng2019}. Permutation quadrature for Shapley values
also uses structured samples \citep{mitchell2022,yang2023}. Our
finite-population target is a paired correctness gap. The lower bounds
identify realizable binary outcomes, and the upper certificates hold
for every real-valued profile on permutations.

Option-order sensitivity is documented in language-model benchmarks
\citep{zheng2024,pez2024}. Position-balanced schedules have been
evaluated on flip rates and pooled choices \citep{ok2026}, and a public
proposal compares Williams and cyclic schedules in multiple-choice
evaluation \citep{lacuna}. Our results concern the worst-case price of
these constraints for a paired gap. \citet{khan2026} study cyclic
and reversed rotations under weight quantization. We randomize their
eight display orders over the labels and compare them at equal cost. Appendix~\ref{app:other-panels}
records additional positive and negative evaluations.

For a fixed question set, the results suggest an explicit choice.
An evaluator who needs a guarantee over arbitrary order effects can
sample either certified position template with its tabulated probability
and relabel it. If independent evidence supports a particular order
effect, a design that balances it removes that component exactly. At four options the optimal sampler depends on the available
budget. At eight, the residual share explains the observed MSE ranking
using all 24 orders. Predicting that share from a small calibration
sample remains open. Other open cases include sharp multi-budget
curves at five or more options, attainment of $L_n$ above ten, and the
five-option correctness optimum with maximum adjacency. The measured benchmark effects condition on the frozen
prompts, models and scoring rules.

\section*{Impact Statement}
The method can reduce the cost of comparative evaluations and make
sensitivity to option order visible. Rankings can still reflect
benchmark coverage, annotation choices, scoring conventions and
model-specific effects. We report exact scope and observed failures
so the measurements can be used with those limits in view.
